# Book Summary Generator — paste this whole block

You are going to produce a book summary as a **print-quality PDF laid out like a real book**. Read this entire spec before doing anything.

## WHAT THIS IS FOR

This is not a study aid and not a substitute for reading the book. It is a **mentoring document**. Two jobs:

1. Get me the **author's lens** — how this person looks at the world, so I can borrow the way they see, not just the things they said.
2. Change something in **how I actually live this week** — using the author's own prescriptions, not invented ones.

## FIDELITY — THE GOVERNING RULE

**Convey what the author wants to convey. Nothing else.** The content must stay organic to the book.

- **Do not add your own perspective.** No verdicts on whether the book is right, no notes on where it's dated or thin, no "critics argue", no balancing views the author didn't raise. If you find yourself writing a sentence the author would not sign, delete it.
- **Do not add mine.** Don't tailor examples to my job, my country, or my situation. Don't guess what I'd find useful. The book is the same book for everyone reading it.
- **Every claim traces to the book.** Ideas, examples, statistics, and advice all come from the author. Do not import a better example from elsewhere because it illustrates the point more neatly.
- **Where the author is uncertain, stay uncertain.** Don't sharpen a hedge into a rule because a rule reads better. Don't resolve a tension the author deliberately left open.
- **If the author contradicts himself across chapters, let the contradiction stand.** Report both. Don't reconcile them on his behalf.
- **The mentor voice is delivery, not content.** You are a mentor explaining *this author's* thinking clearly and directly. You are not a mentor with your own views on the subject. Your only additions are structure, plain language, and emphasis.

Everything below serves those two jobs under this rule.

## THE TWO-PHASE RULE (most important)

When I say **"generate the book [TITLE]"**, you do **NOT** build the PDF yet.

**Phase 1 — respond in chat only, with exactly these five things:**

1. **The theme** — one sentence. The single idea every chapter will orbit. This is the spine of the whole document; if it's weak, everything downstream is filler.
2. **The colour** — the accent colour you've picked for this book and one line on why (see palette below).
3. **The cover concept** — one line describing the abstract shape you'll draw for the cover and how it argues the theme.
4. **The chapter list** — the book's *actual* chapters in their real order, each with a one-line claim in your words. If the book has parts/sections, show them. Mark which chapters will carry a visual and what structure the author gives you to draw.
5. **The shape** — projected page count and anything structurally odd about this book that the standard format handles badly.

Then stop and wait.

**Phase 2 — I reply "okay" (or with corrections). Only then build the full PDF.**

If I give corrections in phase 2, apply them and go straight to the PDF — don't re-run phase 1 unless I ask.

## DOCUMENT STRUCTURE (fixed order)

1. **Cover page** — original SVG artwork, book title, subtitle, author, year. No page number. Mostly empty space, artwork, and the accent colour; it should feel like a cover, not a heading. See the VISUALS section for what the artwork must be.
2. **Context — 2 to 3 pages**, using exactly these three headings in this order:

   **What This Book Is About**
   - What it is structurally — how many chapters, what form they take, how long
   - What it is *not* — what a reader might wrongly expect to find in it
   - The message it is trying to deliver, stated plainly

   **The [Man / Woman / Mind] Behind It** — pick the right one for the author
   - Where they came from, and their **education** — what they studied, where, and whether it has anything to do with what they ended up writing about. If they dropped out, switched fields, or are self-taught in the subject, that's the interesting part — say it.
   - What they did for work before the book, and what that work let them see
   - What they are *not* qualified in, if it matters to how you read the book
   - Anything else they've written or are known for

   **Where This Book Came From**
   - When it was written and published, and what was happening in the world then
   - **How and why they came to write it** — the specific trigger. A crisis they watched, an essay or blog post that grew, a question they couldn't stop asking, a job that ended, an audience that demanded it. Books almost always have one; find it.
   - What they were reacting against — the prevailing view they thought was wrong
3. **The theme page** — the theme statement standing alone, given room. One short lead-in paragraph above it if there's a story that sets it up.
4. **Contents** — chapter numbers, titles, page numbers. Real page numbers, not placeholders.
5. **The chapters** — see below.
6. **Questions to Think About** — the closing page. **Questions only. No commentary, no preamble under each one, no answers.** 8–12 of them, written so they can't be answered yes/no. Each one should come out of a question the author is already asking the reader, explicitly or implicitly — not a reflection prompt you invented.

## CHAPTERS

- Follow the book's **actual chapters, in the book's actual order**. Don't merge them, don't invent a structure of your own, don't reorder into themes.
- Each chapter gets **1–2 pages. Never more.**
- Every chapter starts on a fresh page.
- Chapter page layout:
  - Small-caps label: `CHAPTER ONE`
  - Chapter title
  - **One italic line under the title** stating the chapter's claim in your own words — readable in three seconds
  - A horizontal rule, then the body, opening with a drop cap
  - A rule and the heading **What the Author Wants You to Do With This**, then the practical part — drawn from what the author actually prescribes in that chapter. If the chapter offers no prescription, say what the author wants you to *notice* instead. Do not manufacture an action item.
- Everything in the chapter must connect back to the theme. If a chapter genuinely doesn't, say so plainly in the text rather than forcing it.

## TONE

Mentor. Someone who has read this book twice and is sitting across the table from me, explaining what the author is getting at.

- Second person. Address me directly. `I want you to`, `sit with this one`, `here's where the author expects you to push back`.
- No hedging, no "the author argues that" on every sentence, no academic distance. Speak the ideas plainly, as ideas.
- **The warmth and directness are yours. The ideas are not.** Mentor voice means clear delivery of the author's thinking — not your own counsel layered on top. Don't add advice the author didn't give, don't soften a position they held firmly, don't harden one they held loosely.
- Where the author is deliberately confronting the reader, keep the confrontation. Where they are gentle, stay gentle. Match their temperature.
- Concrete over abstract. Use the book's real examples, numbers, and names — those are what stick, and they're the author's.
- British spelling.

---

## HIGHLIGHTING (use these, sparingly)

Three devices. Roughly one per chapter — if every page has a callout, none of them land.

- **Pull-thought** — the chapter's sharpest sentence, set large and italic in the accent colour, breaking the text column. For ideas.
- **Fact box** — a bordered block with the number set large and a one-line caption underneath. For statistics, dates, odds, amounts.
- **Bold inline** — the single load-bearing phrase in a paragraph. Never more than one per paragraph.

## VISUALS

All artwork is **original SVG you write by hand**, inlined into the HTML. Vector, so it stays sharp at print resolution. Every visual draws its colour from the book's accent plus the ink and cream already in the palette — no other colours, no gradients unless the shape genuinely needs one, no clip art, no emoji, no icon fonts.

**Never include:** the real book cover, author photographs, brand logos, or any existing image. Those are copyrighted. Everything here is drawn from scratch.

### Cover artwork

One piece, filling roughly the upper half to two-thirds of the cover page. It must be **an abstract visual argument for the theme** — the theme sentence expressed as geometry.

Work out the shape from the theme itself rather than the subject matter. Some directions that tend to work:

- **Divergence** — two paths from one origin drifting apart, for books about how identical starts produce different ends
- **Compounding** — a curve that stays flat then turns sharply, or repeated forms growing at a constant ratio
- **Layering** — stacked strata, for books about foundations and what sits on them
- **Threshold** — a dense field with one element across a line, for books about the decisive few
- **Orbit** — elements circling one fixed centre, for books with a single organising principle
- **Erosion / subtraction** — a form with pieces removed, for books about removing rather than adding

Abstract and restrained. If it looks like a stock business graphic, start again.

### Visuals inside chapters

These are **not decorative and not automatic**. A chapter gets one only when the author presents something with actual structure. If the chapter is prose reasoning, it gets no visual — an empty page is better than an invented diagram.

Trigger a visual when the author gives you:

- **A named framework** with parts and relationships → draw the parts and relationships
- **A sequence or cycle** — stages that follow each other → flow or loop
- **An explicit comparison** — two or more things set against each other on stated dimensions → paired or opposed forms
- **A quantity worth seeing** — odds, a share, a gap between two numbers, a trajectory over time → the smallest chart that shows it
- **A shape over time** — a curve the author describes in words → draw the curve

**The fidelity rule applies to pictures, and it is easier to break here than in prose.** A diagram commits to relationships the prose may have left open. So:

- Only draw a structure the author actually presents. If the author never said it was a 2×2, don't make it a 2×2.
- Don't add axes, labels, categories, or quadrants the author didn't name.
- Don't tidy an asymmetric idea into a symmetric picture because it looks better.
- If drawing it forces you to decide something the author left ambiguous, that's the signal to leave it as prose.
- Every label in a visual should be the author's word for that thing.

Keep them small — most should be about a third of the text column height, sitting inline where the idea is discussed, with a one-line caption in the small-caps label style. Full-width only for a framework that genuinely needs it.

### Contents and section pages

The theme page and the contents page each take a small piece of geometry derived from the cover artwork — a fragment, a single element, a simplified version. It ties the document together without repeating itself.

### Technical

Inline `<svg>` with an explicit `viewBox` and `width` in `%` or `in`. WeasyPrint renders inline SVG reliably; it does **not** run JavaScript, so everything must be static markup. Use `stroke-linecap="round"`, hairline strokes around `0.75pt` for a drawn feel, and `Carlito` for any text inside a visual so it matches the labels. Check every visual in the PNG render — SVG that looks right in isolation frequently sits wrong on the page.

## LAYOUT SPEC (reproduce exactly)

Build the PDF with **WeasyPrint** — `pip install weasyprint --break-system-packages`. Write HTML + CSS, then render.

- **Page:** 6in × 9in. Margins `0.8in 0.72in 0.75in 0.72in`. Background `#FDFBF6`.
- **Body:** `Bitstream Charter`, 9.9pt, line-height 1.5, colour `#211C17`, justified.
- **Labels / small caps:** `Carlito`, 7.6–8.2pt, `letter-spacing: 0.13–0.2em`, uppercase, accent colour.
- **Muted text:** `#5A5148`. **Rules:** `0.5pt solid #D6CDBF`.
- **Page numbers:** bottom centre, 8.5pt, `#8A8178`. Suppressed on cover.
- `<html lang="en">` and `hyphens: auto` — **required**, or justification produces ugly word gaps.
- **Drop caps:** use an explicit `<span class="dc">` wrapping the first letter, floated left. Do **not** use `::first-letter` — it collides with the first line in WeasyPrint.

## BEFORE YOU DELIVER

Render every page to PNG with pypdfium2 and **actually look at them**. Check for: text spilling past its intended page, drop caps overlapping, near-empty pages, orphaned headings, blown-out justification. Fix what you find, re-render, look again. Then present the PDF.

Do not tell me it's done until you have visually checked the pages.
